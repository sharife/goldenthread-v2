*As a dramaturgy team member of the fourth partnership between Golden Thread Productions and [Literary Managers and Dramaturgs of the Americas (LMDA)](https://lmda.org/region-info-metro-bay-area/), ReOrient Publications Dramaturg Emily DeDakis contemplates the power of strikes and boycotts while conjuring Henry Street in Dublin – site of Hannah Khalil’s compact, rhythmic masterpiece of a play in ReOrient 2026.*

**ABOUT EMILY DeDAKIS** 
Emily DeDakis (she/her) is a writer and dramaturg from the Southeast U.S. now living in Belfast, Ireland. As a dramaturg, she has developed dozens of scripts and performances in her native and adopted homes, including immersive, 24-hour, ensemble/devised, multimedia, verbatim, and dance theatre. Emily writes for theatre, sound art, radio, TV, and contemporary music, and performs voice/text with HIVE Choir and No Hevdem.

Hannah Khalil’s play *Blood Fruit* takes place on Henry Street, a pedestrianized thoroughfare in the heart of Dublin that’s crammed with life: buskers and street performers, people criss-crossing on foot or bicycle, wee shops, flagship department stores **—** and then there are the icons… On the corner of Henry and O’Connell Streets is the General Post Office, epicenter of the 1916 Easter Rising, where rebels against the British monarchy proclaimed a new Irish republic (even before their cause was a popular one in Ireland). Blood Fruit is dedicated to one of the street’s more subtle icons: the branch of Dunnes department store where in 1984 a young grocery cashier called Mary Manning refused to ring up two grapefruits imported from the Apartheid state of South Africa. Citrus fruit in the hand of an Irish woman launched a trade union strike that led to the first total boycott of South African goods by any Western nation. It’s a familiar story around Ireland (a partner’s uncle worked at Dunnes Stores back then, so I’d heard a secondhand tidbit of the lore already) but it’s not well known further afield. And with all the urgent conversation about boycotts right now, Khalil wants everyone to know about the [Grapefruit Ladies](https://nwlocalpaper.com/david-nihill-grapefruit-story). 

I always feel lucky talking to Hannah Khalil. The way she speaks theatre, frames, and explores it in conversation, is fiercely intriguing and deeply satisfying. Khalil is an accomplished Palestinian-Irish playwright brought up in Dubai and based in London. Her 2019 play *A Museum in Baghdad* marked the Royal Shakespeare Company’s first mainstage production by a woman writer of Arab descent. She’s a familiar voice and an Affiliated Artist at Golden Thread, who produced her *Scenes from 71* Years* in their 2019 season, and her short piece *The Rehearsal* in ReOrient 2017.

Jafari, who has studied playwriting in Tehran and San Francisco, encourages us to think expansively about dramatic storytelling, and to question whether traditional approaches are able to capture everyone’s reality. They note that Western theatrical storytelling with its specific structures and styles “trains us to experience reality in a certain way. What we view as absurdity might just be another way of experiencing reality.” As a playwright using incongruity to critique oppressive systems, Jafari is in the company of luminaries including Vaçlav Havel, Suzan Lori-Parks, and Wole Soyinka. As in the work of these artists, *Homing Pigeons & Co* is full of wit. Reflecting on the play’s lighter moments, Jafari shares, “Humor is how I work around traumatic experiences. Being playful around hard emotions is a way to maintain the characters’ dignity.” While we witness Tina’s and Halan’s victimization, we also recognize them as more than their suffering.

*Homing Pigeons* offers barbed humor in the vein of some of our blackest theatrical comedians: Samuel Beckett, María Irene Fornés, and Sarah Kane. As the danger of Tina’s situation bears down, the young women distract themselves by recalling a chemistry class in which they were asked by their female teacher to carefully observe a still glass of water and “notice” its liquid state. As they listen for the morning Azan (call to prayer), the friends’ rueful reminiscences of the “science experiment” suggest how gender expectations permeate every aspect of the fundamentalist Islamic Republic, from high school curriculum to clothing to the spaces women can occupy. “Chemistry is your maman cooking in the kitchen not learning to make Molotov cocktails,” Tina intones in imitation of their teacher. In this way, the seeming banality of a science lesson is linked to the fines, beatings, sexual assault, incarceration, and murder of women who do not comply with the morality laws of the Islamic Republic. While the former is relatively benign, both the curriculum and the clothing restrictions are disciplinary tactics employed to maintain rigid gender roles. When the Azan finally sounds, Jafari explicitly connects this violence with conservative Islamic doctrine, as Tina tenderly explains to the goldfish, “it’s time to get up and pray but also to get up and hang some people.”

For most Westerners, this is dystopic stuff. The play’s third character is a female cop whose duties “on a normal day” include morality patrolling. The Guidance Patrol, an official branch of the Islamic Republic’s law enforcement wing, makes the rounds of busy spaces, surveilling to enforce dress codes and to disburse groups of mixed genders. While one can argue that the U.S. has morality forces of its own, mobilizing everywhere from school board meetings to the virtual public spheres of Tik Tok and Instagram, they aren’t public servants (yet).

Jafari, who is currently pursuing a PhD in Performance Studies from UC Davis, accepts the characterization of their work as absurd. They cite Ibsen’s perfect five-act play, or conflict between designated protagonists and antagonists, or characters performing specific gender roles, as elements of storytelling that we’ve come to understand and even expect. “This is what you see if you’re always centering a certain kind of writer,” Jafari observes. “But that’s not how I experience my body, my reality. As I saw more non-Western alternatives and leaned into how I genuinely feel things,” they explained, “my writing became weirder.”

*Homing Pigeons & Co* explores borders — borders between nations, bodies, genders, nationalities, races, and even between life and death. Borders signify somewhere we can’t follow, whether due to sociopolitical controls, the laws of the physical world, or a transformation of our metaphysical state. By giving and receiving love, and by “co-parenting,” as Jafari describes it, their pet fish, Tina and Halan challenge the borders that seek to constrain them. Audiences of *Homing Pigeons & Co* may ask themselves: What does care look like when individuals cannot be in the same physical space? How are our bodies, minds, and relationships being shaped by larger forces? Beyond seemingly insurmountable, intolerable, or incomprehensible circumstances, what calls us home?
